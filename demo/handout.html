A language model on an ordinary laptop

What it is, why the industry cares, and where the work leads.

The demonstration. A 2019 laptop with 6.7 GiB of RAM and no discrete graphics card runs a 491 MB language model on its CPU. The Wi-Fi is off. A question is typed; the answer is computed on the machine. Nothing leaves the room. Measured today: 0.29–0.42 s to the first token, then 35–41 tokens per second on the performance power profile; 1.2–1.9 s and 10.0–10.3 tokens per second on power-saver; 600 MB resident. (The larger benchmark numbers below come from the project's receipts, not from the live model.)

Why it matters

  • Privacy. A clinic's patient text stays in the clinic. There is no upload, no vendor and no third-party terms.
  • Cost. After the laptop, each question is free at the margin. A clinic with no cloud budget can still use it.
  • Access. It works offline: a rural clinic, a plane, a room with no reliable network.
  • Control. The code is open and the low-level kernels are being proved correct, so the behaviour can be inspected rather than trusted.

The industry is moving this way. Phones and laptops ship neural hardware (Apple Intelligence, Copilot+ PCs, Snapdragon X, RTX AI PCs), and the competitive question is no longer only "who has the biggest model" but "who can make a useful model fit and run fast on the device the person already owns". The bottleneck is memory: a 7B model at 4 bits is about 4 GB and needs a new machine; at 1.17 bits it is under 1 GB and fits the one already in the clinic.

For a clinician, the near-term uses are administrative: drafting a letter, summarising an intake note, turning bullet points into plain language. The clinician edits and signs; the model never decides. The reason to run it locally is that the draft contains patient text.

Plain terms

  • Parameter. A number the model learned. "7B" means seven billion of them.
  • Bit. One eighth of a byte. Fewer bits per parameter means a smaller model; 1.17 bits per parameter is the project's compression target.
  • Token. A word piece; roughly 0.75 words. "20 tokens per second" is about 15 words per second, faster than most people read aloud.
  • Context. The text the model can hold in view at once. 200,000 tokens is roughly a 500-page book.
  • Q4_K_M. A standard 4-bit compression used by most local models. The quality bar is to stay within 15% of it.
  • Lean. A proof assistant. The low-level arithmetic is checked by a machine, not by eye.

Why it is a career

Three scarce skills meet in this work:

  • Machine-learning systems — making a model fit and run fast, not training another one.
  • Performance engineering — memory bandwidth, quantisation, CPU/GPU kernels, measurement.
  • Formal verification — proving the low-level code does what it claims, in Lean.

Most candidates have one. The combination is the differentiator, and this repository is the portfolio: code, measurements, receipts and proofs that a hiring manager can run. The roles are inference and ML-systems engineer, performance engineer, kernel or compiler engineer, research engineer for efficient ML, quant developer, and edge or privacy-preserving AI. Employers include chip companies, labs, cloud providers, inference startups, finance and health technology.

Indicative senior compensation is roughly A$180k–300k in Australia and US$200k–400k+ total compensation in the United States. It varies with company, level and location; it is not a promise.

The next steps are concrete: finish and publish the 1.15–1.18 b/w pack; write the "1.17 bits per weight" report; give a talk; contribute a kernel or benchmark to an open-source inference project; apply to teams that measure tokens per second and bytes per weight.

What it is not

It is not a therapist, a diagnosis or a medical device. A clinician stays in the loop: the model drafts, the human decides. The value is privacy, cost, access and control — not replacing judgment.
